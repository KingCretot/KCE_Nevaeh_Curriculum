Before you read: a joke with a serious point

In 1729, Jonathan Swift published an essay suggesting that poor Irish families sell their babies to rich people as food.

No. The whole essay is satire. The narrator, the "I," is a calm, polite "expert" Swift invented so he could mock people who treated the poor like numbers. The more reasonable the narrator sounds, the more horrible the idea should feel.

So, when the narrator sounds calm and sensible, Swift wants you to feel...

The calm tone makes the cruelty stand out. That contrast is the heart of satire.

Ireland in 1729

Who ruled?tap
England. A small group of Protestant landlords, many living in England, owned most of the land.
Who suffered?tap
Poor Catholic farmers who rented land. Rents were high, crops failed, and families begged in the streets.
Who was Swift?tap
An Irish-born priest in Dublin and author of Gulliver's Travels, angry at how the poor were treated.

Who is Swift really angry at?

The poor are the victims. Swift's target is the people with power over them.

The three appeals, and how Swift twists them

Writers persuade with three appeals. Read each definition, then tap the card to see how Swift turns it against his narrator.

LogosAn appeal to logic: facts, numbers, statistics and step-by-step reasoning.tap: how Swift twists it
Swift's twist: the narrator does careful math about babies and shillings (¶6, ¶14, ¶23). The more logical it sounds, the more monstrous it feels.
EthosAn appeal to credibility: the speaker proves he is trustworthy, fair and knows his subject.tap: how Swift twists it
Swift's twist: the narrator says he studied the problem for years and gains nothing himself (¶4, ¶33). A "trustworthy" man is proposing something horrifying.
PathosAn appeal to emotion: the writer makes you feel pity, fear or anger.tap: how Swift twists it
Swift's twist: the narrator shows almost no feeling about suffering (¶19). His coldness makes the reader feel the emotion he is missing.

The narrator computes that a child costs 2 shillings and sells for 10. Which appeal is that?

Numbers and calculations are logos.

When the narrator says he gains nothing because his own children are grown (¶33), he is using...

He is building credibility, which is ethos, but it's ironic because he risks nothing.

¶1–7: the problem

What the narrator says

Ireland's streets are full of mothers begging with several children. Whoever finds a cheap way to make these children “useful” deserves a statue. His math says 120,000 poor babies are born each year, with no jobs or land for them.

The suffering is real. But calling hungry children a “prodigious number” and a “grievance” shows the narrator sees people as a problem to solve, not as human beings.
¶6“...two hundred thousand couple whose wives are breeders...”
The numbers sound researched (logos), but “breeders” treats poor women like livestock.

What does the narrator's math in ¶6 do?

Statistics make the plan sound believable. That's the trick Swift is exposing.

¶8–13: the “modest” proposal

What the narrator says

He “humbly” proposes selling poor one-year-olds as food for the rich. Keep 20,000 for breeding and sell 100,000. Babies are “dear” (expensive), so they suit landlords.

This is the shock moment, and ¶12 reveals the real target: the landlords have already “devoured” the parents by taking their rent, food and land. The rich are already eating the poor alive, just slowly and legally.
¶8“I shall now therefore humbly propose my own thoughts, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection.”
Verbal irony: the most outrageous idea is called humble and impossible to object to.
¶12“...very proper for landlords, who, as they have already devoured most of the parents, seem to have the best title to the children.”
“Devoured” is a metaphor for taking everything from tenants.

In ¶12, “devoured” really means the landlords...

It's a metaphor. Swift is accusing the landlords of exploitation.

¶14–17: profits, recipes and “advice”

What the narrator says

A beggar's child costs 2 shillings a year and sells for 10. The mother earns 8 shillings, the skin makes gloves, and Dublin can open butcher shops. He rejects eating older kids because the meat is tough and some might call it “a little bordering upon cruelty.”

Business words, profit and recipes show how the rich see the poor: as products. “A little bordering upon cruelty” is understatement. Eating children is far beyond “a little” cruel.
¶17“...some scrupulous people might be apt to censure such a practice... as a little bordering upon cruelty...”
Swift wants you to react: “a LITTLE cruel?!”

“A little bordering upon cruelty” is an example of...

It makes something horrifying sound minor.

¶18–20: understatement

What the narrator says

He tells a story of executed bodies sold as delicacies. He isn't worried about the old and sick poor, because they are dying of cold and hunger “as fast as can be reasonably expected.”

Swift attacks people who were truly that indifferent to the dying poor. Treating mass death as calm good news is the strongest understatement in the essay.
¶19“...they are every day dying, and rotting, by cold and famine, and filth, and vermin, as fast as can be reasonably expected.”
Horrible suffering described like a delivery that's on schedule.

Why does the narrator say this so calmly?

The cold tone exposes the indifference of the powerful.

¶21–26: the six “advantages”

Each “benefit” secretly accuses someone. Tap a row to see who.

¶21 There will be fewer Catholics.
Really criticizing: Protestant leaders who hated Irish Catholics.
¶22 Poor tenants will finally own something valuable to pay rent.
Really criticizing: Landlords who already took their crops and cattle.
¶23 The nation earns money and gets a new dish.
Really criticizing: An economy that only counts the wealthy.
¶24 Parents earn money and stop paying to raise kids.
Really criticizing: A system that treats children as a cost.
¶25 Taverns get customers and cooks charge more.
Really criticizing: Greedy businesses that profit from anything.
¶26 Husbands will treat pregnant wives better, like pregnant cows.
Really criticizing: Men who abused their wives, and seeing people as livestock.

Listing the benefits like a business plan makes the essay...

The organized list makes a monstrous idea look like normal policy.

¶27–30: the twist hiding in the list

In ¶29 the narrator says “let no man talk to me of other expedients” and lists ideas he refuses to hear. Tap each one: what does Swift actually want?

Taxing absentees at five shillings a pound
Tax landlords who live in England.
Using only clothes and furniture made in Ireland
Buy Irish to support Irish workers.
Curing pride, vanity, idleness and gambling
The rich should stop wasting money.
Quitting animosities and factions
The Irish should unite instead of fighting.
Teaching landlords one degree of mercy
Landlords should treat tenants humanely.
Honest shopkeepers
Merchants should stop cheating.

So the “other expedients” in ¶29 are...

This is the most important paragraph: the ideas he rejects are exactly what Swift wants.

¶31–33: the ending

What the narrator says

His plan costs nothing and won't upset England. Critics should ask poor parents whether they'd rather have been eaten at age one than suffer for life. He swears he gains nothing: his youngest child is nine and his wife can't have more.

The ending sounds unselfish (ethos) but it's ironic: he is safe because his own family can never be sold. The question about the parents forces readers to face how miserable the poor's lives already are.
¶31“...although perhaps I could name a country, which would be glad to eat up our whole nation without it.”
The country is England, already consuming Ireland.
¶33“I have no children, by which I can propose to get a single penny...”
He claims to be selfless, but he risks nothing.

Why does he mention his own children are too old?

Ironic ethos: he sounds trustworthy only because he has nothing at stake.

Big ideas (themes)

Tap each theme.

Greed and exploitationtap
The rich profit from the poor until nothing is left. Swift makes it literal.
Dehumanizationtap
Talk about people as numbers and meat, and it's easy to ignore them.
Cold logictap
An argument can sound logical and still be evil.
Satire as actiontap
Shock readers so they'll finally accept real reforms.

Which theme do the six “advantages” build most?

Every benefit profits the powerful at the cost of the poor.
