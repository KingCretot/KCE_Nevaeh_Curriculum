Study Guide: “A Modest Proposal”

Jonathan Swift, 1729 · Nevaeh's English I, Lesson 3 · Ask every paragraph: What does he SAY? What does Swift MEAN? Who is he really attacking?

Before you read: this is a joke with a serious point

In 1729, Jonathan Swift published a pamphlet suggesting that poor Irish families sell their babies to rich people as food. He did not mean it. The whole essay is satire: it pretends to be a serious, sensible plan so readers feel shocked, and then realize the real horror is how the poor in Ireland were actually being treated.

The narrator, the "I" in the essay, is a character Swift invented: a calm, polite, numbers-loving "expert." Swift is mocking that kind of person. Every time the narrator sounds reasonable, Swift wants you to feel how unreasonable it is.

In this lesson you will learn the background, every hard word, what each section really means, the satire tools Swift uses, and how to answer the questions in your packet. The bar at the top stays with you the whole time.

Background: Ireland in 1729

Who ruled

England controlled Ireland. A small group of Protestant landowners, many living in England, owned most of the land. The English government also limited what Ireland could sell to other countries.

Who suffered

Most Irish people were poor Catholic farmers who rented land. Rents were high, crops had failed for several years, and many families were starving and begging in the streets.

Who wrote it

Jonathan Swift was an Irish-born Protestant priest, the head of St. Patrick's Cathedral in Dublin, and the author of Gulliver's Travels. He was angry at how England and the landlords treated the Irish poor.

Why this matters

Once you know the landlords were taking everything from poor families, a line like "landlords... have already devoured most of the parents" (paragraph 12) stops being about eating and starts being about money and power.

The satire toolkit

Satire

Writing that uses humor, exaggeration and irony to attack a real problem or people in power. The goal is change, not just laughs.

Verbal irony

Saying the opposite of what you mean. The narrator calls his plan "modest" and "humble." It is the least modest idea imaginable.

Persona

A made-up speaker. The narrator is not Swift; he is the kind of cold, greedy planner Swift wants to expose.

Understatement

Describing something terrible as if it were small or normal, like poor people dying "as fast as can be reasonably expected" (paragraph 19).

Hyperbole

Extreme exaggeration. The whole proposal is hyperbole: it takes the way the rich already "use up" the poor and pushes it to the most extreme version.

Dehumanizing language

Words for animals or meat used for people: "breeders," "dam," "carcass," "stock." Swift uses them to show how the powerful see the poor.

Logos, ethos and pathos, twisted

Your packet asks about rhetorical appeals. Swift uses all three, but he flips each one so it backfires on the narrator.

AppealWhat it normally doesHow Swift twists it
Logos
logic		Facts, numbers and reasoning make an argument convincing.The narrator does careful math about babies, pounds and shillings (paragraphs 6, 10, 14, 23, 28). The more logical it sounds, the more monstrous it feels.
Ethos
credibility		The speaker shows he is trustworthy and fair.He says he has studied the problem "for many years," quotes "experts," and swears he gains nothing (paragraphs 4, 29, 33). A trustworthy-sounding man is proposing cannibalism.
Pathos
emotion		The writer makes you feel pity or anger.The narrator shows almost no feeling while describing horror. That cold tone is what makes the reader feel the emotion he is missing.
So the answer to "how does Swift use appeals to build irony?" includes all three working together.

Paragraphs 1–7: the problem

What the narrator says

Ireland is full of poor mothers begging with three to six children. Whoever finds a cheap, easy way to make these children useful deserves a statue. He has done the math: about 120,000 poor babies are born each year, and there are no jobs, no land and no way to raise them.

What Swift means

The suffering is real, and Swift describes it honestly at first. Calling hungry children a "prodigious number" and a "grievance" already shows the narrator thinks of people as a problem to solve, not as human beings.

PARAGRAPH 1“It is a melancholy object to those, who walk through this great town...”

What it really means: The narrator starts with sadness, so he seems caring. Watch how quickly that care turns into math.

PARAGRAPH 6“...two hundred thousand couple whose wives are breeders...”

What it really means: The numbers make him sound researched (logos), but "breeders" shows he sees poor women as livestock.

Paragraphs 8–13: the “modest” proposal

What the narrator says

He "humbly" proposes that at one year old, poor children be sold as food to the rich. Keep 20,000 for breeding and sell the other 100,000. A child makes two dishes for a party. Babies are "dear" (expensive), so they are perfect for landlords.

What Swift means

This is the shock moment. The real target appears in paragraph 12: the landlords have already "devoured" the parents by taking their rent, food and land. Swift is saying the rich are already eating the poor alive, just slowly and legally.

PARAGRAPH 8“I shall now therefore humbly propose my own thoughts, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection.”

What it really means: Verbal irony: the most outrageous idea in the essay is called humble and impossible to object to.

PARAGRAPH 12“...very proper for landlords, who, as they have already devoured most of the parents, seem to have the best title to the children.”

What it really means: "Devoured" is a metaphor for exploiting tenants. This line tells you who Swift is really angry at.

Paragraphs 14–17: profits, recipes and “advice”

What the narrator says

A beggar's child costs two shillings a year to keep but sells for ten. The squire becomes a popular landlord, the mother earns eight shillings, the skin makes gloves and boots, and Dublin can open special butcher shops. A friend suggested eating 12- to 14-year-olds too, but the narrator rejects it because the meat would be tough and some people might call it "a little bordering upon cruelty."

What Swift means

Swift piles up business language, profit and recipes to show how the rich see the poor: as products. The joke about "a little bordering upon cruelty" is understatement: eating children is far beyond "a little" cruel.

PARAGRAPH 14“...the mother will have eight shillings neat profit, and be fit for work till she produces another child.”

What it really means: A mother is described like a farm animal producing income. That is the exaggerated (hyperbole) logos Swift is mocking.

PARAGRAPH 17“...some scrupulous people might be apt to censure such a practice... as a little bordering upon cruelty...”

What it really means: Understatement on purpose: Swift wants you to say out loud, "a LITTLE cruel?"

Paragraphs 18–20: understatement

What the narrator says

He tells a story about a man from Formosa who sold executed bodies as delicacies. He adds that he is not worried about the old, sick and disabled poor, because they are dying of cold, hunger and disease "as fast as can be reasonably expected."

What Swift means

Swift attacks people who were truly that indifferent to the dying poor. Treating mass death as calm good news is the clearest example of understatement in the essay.

PARAGRAPH 19“...they are every day dying, and rotting, by cold and famine, and filth, and vermin, as fast as can be reasonably expected.”

What it really means: Horrible suffering is described like an on-schedule delivery. That cold tone is the satire.

Paragraphs 21–26: the six “advantages”

The narrator lists six benefits, like a business plan. Each one quietly accuses a real group.

#What the narrator saysWho Swift is really criticizing
1 (¶21)There will be fewer Catholics.Protestant leaders who hated Irish Catholics.
2 (¶22)Poor tenants will finally own something valuable to pay rent with.Landlords who had already taken their crops and cattle.
3 (¶23)The nation earns money and gets a new dish for rich tables.An economy that only counts the wealthy.
4 (¶24)Parents make money and no longer pay to raise kids.A system that makes children a cost instead of a future.
5 (¶25)Taverns get new customers and cooks can charge more.Greedy businesses that profit from anything.
6 (¶26)Husbands will treat pregnant wives better, like a farmer treats a pregnant cow.Men who abused their wives, and treating people like livestock.

The theme this builds

Greed and exploitation of the poor have become so normal that a plan to eat children can be listed like ordinary profits.

Paragraphs 27–30: the twist hiding in the list

In paragraph 29 the narrator says, “let no man talk to me of other expedients,” and then lists the ideas he refuses to hear. Those “other expedients” are Swift's real solutions. This is the most important paragraph in the essay.

What he lists (¶29)In plain English: what Swift actually wants
Taxing absentees at five shillings a poundTax the landlords who live in England and drain Ireland's money.
Using only clothes and furniture made in IrelandBuy Irish products to help Irish workers.
Rejecting foreign luxuryStop wasting money on expensive imports.
Curing pride, vanity, idleness and gamblingThe wealthy should stop wasting money on themselves.
Learning to love our country; quitting animosities and factionsIrish people should unite instead of fighting each other.
Teaching landlords one degree of mercyLandlords should treat tenants humanely.
Honest shopkeepersMerchants should stop cheating customers.

Why it matters

Paragraph 30 adds that these ideas will never happen until someone sincerely tries them. Swift is saying the sensible solutions were ignored for years, so he wrote something shocking to make people pay attention.

Paragraphs 31–33: the ending

What the narrator says

He is tired of offering useless ideas. His plan costs nothing and won't upset England. If anyone has a better plan, they should first ask poor parents whether they would rather have been eaten at age one than suffer their whole lives. He swears he has no personal interest: his youngest child is nine and his wife is too old to have more.

What Swift means

The final lines use ethos ("I gain nothing, I only want the public good"), but they are ironic: he is safe because none of his own family could ever be sold. The question about the parents forces readers to face how miserable the poor's lives already are, which is Swift's real purpose.

PARAGRAPH 31“...although perhaps I could name a country, which would be glad to eat up our whole nation without it.”

What it really means: The "country" is England. Swift is saying England is already consuming Ireland.

PARAGRAPH 33“I have no children, by which I can propose to get a single penny...”

What it really means: He claims to be unselfish, but he risks nothing. The plan only ever hurts the poor, never people like him.

Big ideas (themes) to know

Greed and exploitation

The rich and the landlords profit from the poor until there is nothing left. Swift shows this by making the exploitation literal.

Dehumanization

When people are talked about as numbers, "breeders" and "carcasses," it becomes easy to ignore their suffering.

Cold logic without morality

An argument can sound logical and still be evil. Numbers alone don't make something right.

Indifference of the powerful

Leaders and landlords ignored real solutions for years. Swift attacks that indifference.

Satire as a call to action

Shock is the tool. Swift wants readers so disturbed that they finally accept the real reforms in paragraph 29.
