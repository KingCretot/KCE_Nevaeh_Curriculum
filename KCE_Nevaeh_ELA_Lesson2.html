Back to Chapter 1 — on purpose

You've already read past this chapter in class — that's fine. This isn't about re-reading the plot. It's about learning to see what's actually going on underneath it, which is a different skill from just following the story. The bar above stays pinned the whole lesson.

By the end of today, you'll know what an allegory actually is, how to spot one, and exactly what Chapter 1 is quietly setting up for everything that happens later.

What is an "allegory," really?

Allegory

A story where the characters and events stand for something else entirely — usually a real historical event or a big idea — so the reader can see that thing more clearly than if it were just explained directly.

a farm animal, standing in for something real

Think about it like this: if a story wants to make a point about a real dictator, it's often more powerful to show a different kind of ruler doing the exact same things — because you're not distracted by what you already think about the real person. That's exactly what George Orwell is doing with farm animals. Animal Farm isn't really about a farm. It's about the Russian Revolution and what happened to it afterward.

How to actually read an allegory

1. Who does this remind me of?

In real life or history — which figure does this character map onto?

2. What real event is this?

What moment in the story could represent something that actually happened?

3. What's the author really saying?

Why tell it this way instead of just saying it directly?

Use this on literally every chapter from here on. It's the lens for the entire book.

What Orwell was actually writing about

In the early 1900s, Russia was ruled by a Tsar — an unelected king with total power. In 1917, a revolution overthrew him, promising a fairer system where power belonged to the workers. Instead, over the next few decades, the country ended up under Joseph Stalin, a dictator arguably just as controlling as the Tsar — just with different propaganda.

That's the real story Animal Farm is retelling with animals: a group overthrows an unfair ruler in the name of equality for everyone — and slowly, quietly, ends up right back under a different kind of unfair rule.

Chapter 1: what happens

An old, well-respected pig calls the other animals together late one night. He tells them their lives are miserable and short because they're being used by the farm's human owner — and lays out a vision of what life could look like if the animals ran the farm themselves, with no humans involved at all. He teaches them a song about that future. Then, not long after, he dies — before any of it actually happens.

Notice what's missing

This chapter never shows the idea actually being put into practice. It's pure vision, no reality check yet. That matters a lot for later.

Who is Old Major, really?

Old Major
The founding revolutionary IDEA itself — echoes of thinkers like Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin, who imagined and helped launch the real revolution.
Old Major isn't a villain, and he's not really a full "character" who does things later — he's the ideal itself, the pure vision before anyone has to actually run anything. Orwell keeps him spotless, because the whole point of the book is watching what happens to a good idea once real people start putting it into practice.

Two words you'll need constantly

Tyranny

Unfair, cruel rule by someone with way more power than everyone else, and no real way to stop them.

Propaganda

Information — sometimes true, often twisted or false — designed to make you FEEL a certain way about something, so you believe it without really questioning it. Think of an ad that skips the facts and just makes you feel excited or scared.

Old Major's speech isn't propaganda yet — it's genuinely his honest belief. Watch for the moment later when other characters use the SAME emotional techniques to make animals believe things that aren't actually true. That's the shift from vision to manipulation.

One question worth sitting with

Old Major names the animals' hard, short, exhausting lives as the reason change is needed. Why do you think naming a problem out loud — even before anyone has a real plan to fix it — has that much power over a group of people?

This one doesn't have a single right answer — it's meant to be talked through live, not graded.
