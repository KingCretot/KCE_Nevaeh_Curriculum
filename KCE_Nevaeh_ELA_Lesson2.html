Getting ready for the Chapters 1–8 test

Your test covers the first eight chapters of Animal Farm. This review walks through every chapter, who each character really stands for, how the Seven Commandments get changed, and the big ideas your teacher is most likely to ask about. The bar above stays pinned the whole lesson.

The one idea that ties all eight chapters together: the animals overthrow an unfair ruler in the name of equality, and then, step by step, the pigs take that power for themselves. Watch for that pattern on every slide.

Chapters 1–2: the dream and the Rebellion

Chapter 1

  • Old Major gathers the animals and tells them their lives are miserable because humans use them.
  • He describes a future where animals run the farm, and teaches them the song Beasts of England.

History link: Marx's and Lenin's ideas about workers rising up.

Chapter 2

  • Old Major dies a few nights later. The pigs (Snowball, Napoleon, Squealer) turn his ideas into a system called Animalism.
  • Jones neglects the animals, and they chase him off the farm. They rename it Animal Farm and paint the Seven Commandments on the barn wall.
  • The cows' milk disappears. This is the first sign the pigs are keeping extra for themselves.

History link: The 1917 Russian Revolution, with Animalism standing in for communism.

Chapters 3–4: early success and the first battle

Chapter 3

  • The harvest is a success. Boxer works harder than anyone, and the pigs supervise instead of working.
  • Snowball sets up committees and teaches the sheep a simple slogan: four legs good, two legs bad.
  • Napoleon quietly takes nine puppies away to "educate" them himself.
  • The pigs keep the milk and apples. Squealer says pigs need them for brainwork, and warns that otherwise Jones would come back.

History link: The new leaders start enjoying privileges the workers don't get.

Chapter 4

  • Jones and other men attack the farm to take it back. This is the Battle of the Cowshed.
  • Snowball leads the defense bravely and is wounded. The animals win, and Snowball and Boxer receive medals.

History link: The Russian Civil War, when foreign countries tried to help bring back the old government.

Chapters 5–6: Napoleon takes over

Chapter 5

  • Mollie leaves the farm for a life of ribbons and sugar with humans.
  • Snowball and Napoleon argue about everything, especially Snowball's plan to build a windmill.
  • Napoleon's dogs, the puppies he raised, chase Snowball off the farm. Napoleon ends the Sunday debates.
  • Squealer later claims the windmill was Napoleon's idea all along. Boxer adopts a new motto: Napoleon is always right.

History link: Stalin forcing Trotsky out of the Soviet Union and taking total control.

Chapter 6

  • The animals work extremely long weeks on the windmill. "Voluntary" Sunday work comes with cut food rations for anyone who skips it.
  • Napoleon begins trading with humans through a lawyer, Mr. Whymper, which goes against what the animals agreed to.
  • The pigs move into the farmhouse and sleep in beds, and a commandment is quietly changed.
  • A storm knocks down the windmill. Napoleon blames Snowball.

History link: Stalin's Five-Year Plans: harsh forced labor for big building projects.

Chapters 7–8: fear, lies, and betrayal

Chapter 7

  • A hard winter brings a food shortage. Napoleon hides it from the outside world by filling the food bins with sand.
  • The hens rebel when ordered to give up their eggs. Napoleon cuts off their food, and nine hens die.
  • Snowball is blamed for everything, and Squealer even rewrites the history of the Battle of the Cowshed.
  • Animals "confess" to working with Snowball and the dogs kill them. Beasts of England is banned.

History link: The Soviet famine, Stalin's crackdown on peasants, and the Great Purge with its show trials.

Chapter 8

  • Napoleon is now rarely seen and is given grand titles and poems of praise.
  • He sells timber to Frederick, who pays with fake money and then attacks the farm, blowing up the windmill.
  • The pigs find whisky. Napoleon gets sick, and a commandment is changed again.
  • Squealer is found at night beside a broken ladder near the barn wall, with a can of paint.

History link: Stalin's 1939 deal with Hitler, broken when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941.

How the Seven Commandments change

The commandments start as the rules everyone agrees to. By Chapter 8, the pigs have quietly rewritten three of them to excuse what they are already doing, and the animals can't remember the original wording well enough to prove it.

Sleeping in beds (Chapter 6)

The rule against beds now forbids only beds with sheets, after the pigs move into the farmhouse.

Killing other animals (Chapter 8)

The rule against killing now forbids only killing without cause, after the executions in Chapter 7.

Drinking alcohol (Chapter 8)

The rule against alcohol now forbids only drinking to excess, after the pigs find the whisky.

Why it matters

Changing the rules in secret shows how leaders can rewrite the truth when people can't read well or can't check the record. That is exactly what the ladder at the end of Chapter 8 reveals.

How Napoleon keeps control

Propaganda

Squealer twists facts, numbers, and memories so the animals believe things are better than they are.

Fear

The dogs guard Napoleon and carry out the executions. Animals stop speaking up.

A scapegoat

Snowball gets blamed for every problem, so no one blames Napoleon.

Rewriting history

The commandments and the story of the Battle of the Cowshed are changed to fit what the pigs want.

Loyal workers

Boxer's trust and the sheep's chanting keep anyone from questioning the leaders.

Big ideas your test may ask about

Power corrupts

The pigs start out fighting for equality and end up acting more and more like Mr. Jones.

Language as a weapon

Whoever controls the words, slogans, and records controls what everyone believes.

Knowledge is power

The pigs can read and write well. Most animals can't, so they can't check what the pigs tell them.

Blind trust is dangerous

Boxer's loyalty and the sheep's chanting make it easy for Napoleon to do whatever he wants.

Every word you need

Allegory
A story where characters and events stand for something real, usually history or a big idea.
Animalism
The animals' system based on Old Major's ideas. It stands for communism.
Propaganda
Information designed to make you feel a certain way so you believe it without questioning it.
Tyranny
Cruel, unfair rule with no real way to stop it.
Scapegoat
Someone blamed for problems they didn't cause.
Purge
Getting rid of people a leader sees as a threat, often violently.
Irony
When what happens is the opposite of what was expected, like the pigs becoming just like Jones.

This mirrors Stalin forcing Leon Trotsky out of the Soviet Union. After this, Napoleon blames Snowball for everything that goes wrong.

What is Squealer's main job on the farm?

Squealer is the propaganda machine. He twists facts and numbers so the animals accept whatever the pigs decide.

6 of 10
Boxer's two personal mottoes, "I will work harder" and "Napoleon is always right," show...

Boxer represents the loyal, hardworking working class who trust the leaders completely.

7 of 10
Why do the hens rebel in Chapter 7?

Napoleon cuts off their food until they give in, and nine hens die. This mirrors Stalin's brutal treatment of peasants who resisted him.

8 of 10
The forced confessions and executions in Chapter 7 represent...

Animals "confess" to working with Snowball and are killed by the dogs, just as Stalin had people falsely confess and then executed them.

9 of 10

Napoleon's deal with Frederick mirrors Stalin's 1939 pact with Hitler, which Hitler broke when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941.

At the end of Chapter 8, Squealer is found at night beside a broken ladder near the barn wall. What does this show?

The commandments keep "changing" because the pigs are rewriting them. The animals' weak memory lets the pigs get away with it.
