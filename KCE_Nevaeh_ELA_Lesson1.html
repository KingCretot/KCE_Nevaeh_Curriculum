Proving you actually read it

Anyone can drop a link in a Works Cited list. An annotated bibliography is the assignment where you have to prove you actually engaged with what you found — not just that you found it. Today we're building that skill from the ground up. The bar above stays pinned the whole lesson.

What even counts as a "source"?

Source

Anywhere information comes from that you use as evidence — a book, an article, a website, an interview. If you learned something from it and you're using that in your work, it's a source.

Citation

A short, exact-format note telling a reader precisely where information came from — so they could go find it themselves.

a citation is like a receipt

MLA citation format, piece by piece

Author Last, First. "Title of Source." Container/Publisher, Date.

This is the exact skeleton your teacher is grading against. Every piece is doing a job.

Author
Who's responsible for the information — a person, or an organization if no person is named.
Title
The exact name of the thing you read, in quotation marks.
Container
The larger thing it lives inside — a website, a magazine, a publisher.
Date
When it was published, so a reader knows how current it is.

A real citation, broken into its parts

Reyes, Marco. "Five Things Every New Driver Should Know Before a Long Road Trip." Family Travel Weekly, 14 Mar. 2025.
Author
Reyes, Marco.
Title
"Five Things Every New Driver Should Know..."
Container
Family Travel Weekly
Date
14 Mar. 2025
This is a made-up example — but built exactly like a real one. If you were researching road trip safety for a project, this is precisely how you'd cite it.

A citation alone isn't an annotated bibliography

Annotation

A short paragraph, written by YOU, right after the citation — explaining what the source says and whether it's actually worth trusting or using. This is the part that proves you read it.

anyone can copy a format — an annotation can't be faked

Every annotation has two jobs

SUMMARIZE it  +  EVALUATE it

Skip either half and the annotation is incomplete, even if the citation itself is perfect.

1. Summarize
In 2–3 sentences: what does this source actually say? What's its main point?
2. Evaluate
In 1–2 sentences: is it reliable? Who wrote it, and are they credible? Is it useful or biased or outdated?

Putting the whole entry together

Reyes, Marco. "Five Things Every New Driver Should Know Before a Long Road Trip." Family Travel Weekly, 14 Mar. 2025.
Summary
Lists five practical tips for teen and new drivers on long family trips, including checking tire pressure, planning rest stops every two hours, and keeping an emergency kit in the trunk.
Evaluation
Reyes is a certified driving instructor, which makes the advice credible, and the tips are specific enough to actually use. Published in 2025, so the information is current.
That's a complete entry: citation, summary, evaluation. Nothing extra, nothing missing.

The question every evaluation is really asking

Signs it's reliable

Author is named and has real expertise. Published by a known outlet. Recent, or clearly still accurate. Backed by evidence, not just opinion.

Signs to be cautious

No author listed, no credentials given. Trying to sell you something. Very outdated for a fast-changing topic. Only one side of a debate, presented as fact.

What loses points on this assignment

Summarizing without evaluating. Restating what the source says isn't enough — you still have to say whether it's trustworthy and useful.

Evaluating without summarizing. Saying "this source is reliable" means nothing if the reader has no idea what it's actually about.

Listing sources you didn't really read. A vague, generic summary is usually the giveaway — annotations should sound specific to that one source.

This connects straight to Animal Farm

Animal Farm is an allegory for the Russian Revolution — and at some point, your class work is going to ask you to bring in outside historical background to support that reading.

The moment you cite a source about Stalin, the Russian Revolution, or propaganda, you'll need exactly this same skill: a correct citation, plus a summary and evaluation of whether it's trustworthy.
